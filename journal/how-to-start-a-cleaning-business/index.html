Getting started

How to start a cleaning business (without burning out in year one)

By Lauren Kern
November 1, 2026 · 3 min read

Starting a cleaning business is one of the most accessible ways to work for yourself. You don't need a storefront, a big loan, or a degree. But "easy to start" isn't the same as "easy to run well," and a lot of new owners find that out the hard way.

Here's how I'd start again if I were doing it today, in the order that actually matters.

1. Decide what you want the business to give you

Before you register a name or buy a single bottle of cleaner, get clear on what you want. More income? Flexible hours? A team that runs without you someday? Your answers shape every decision after this one: how many clients you take, how you price, and when you hire.

Write down what "enough" looks like for you. Not what the industry says success is. What you need.

2. Make it official

Getting legal is less scary than it sounds. Most new cleaning businesses need:

  • A business name and registration with your state (many owners start as an LLC).
  • An EIN from the IRS. It's free and takes a few minutes online.
  • A separate business bank account, so your numbers stay clean from the start.
  • General liability insurance. Clients will ask, and it protects you when something breaks.
  • Any local licenses your city or county requires.

Rules vary by state and city, so check with your Secretary of State's office and a local CPA. This isn't legal or tax advice, just the checklist I wish I'd had.

3. Set your prices before you book anyone

The fastest way to burn out is to fill your calendar with jobs that barely pay. Figure out your costs first, then set prices that pay you and leave room to hire later. I walk through the whole process in how to price house cleaning so you actually get paid.

4. Pick your services, and say no to the rest

You don't have to offer everything. Start with the services you can do well and price confidently, like recurring standard cleans and first-time deep cleans. Saying no to the odd jobs keeps your schedule simple and your quality high.

5. Land your first clients

Your first clients usually come from people who already know you. After that, a few simple channels go a long way:

  • Friends, family, and neighbors (tell everyone what you're doing)
  • Local Facebook groups and Nextdoor
  • A Google Business Profile with real photos and reviews
  • Asking every happy client for a referral and a review

Push for recurring clients, weekly or biweekly, from day one. One-time cleans pay the bills this week. Recurring clients build a business.

6. Set up simple systems early

It's tempting to run everything from your phone's notes app and text messages. It works until it doesn't. A few systems early on save you hours every week:

  • A cleaning checklist, so every home gets the same standard
  • Client notes for gate codes, pets, and preferences
  • Online booking, reminders, and payments on file

I run all of this through Jobber. It schedules recurring jobs, sends branded estimates, and charges clients automatically after each visit. You can try it free for 30 days with my link.

7. Plan your first hire before you need it

If your business depends on your body holding up, it's fragile. Start thinking about help early, even if you're not ready yet. Decide whether 1099 subcontractors or W2 employees fit your goals (I break down the pros and cons of each), and keep your pricing high enough to pay someone else to do the work.

Start small, start smart

You don't need to have it all figured out on day one. You need a clear goal, prices that work, and a few good clients. Build from there, one real step at a time.
